Ruminance Blog #002 - October 2, 2026

Hello, again. This week's is brought to you by some albums and artists I've been exploring to broaden the scope of my storytelling. I've deliberately avoided lyrical music. I can't have Tool's "Descending" informing every decision every character makes by virtue of being the "sweet spot" around which point I enter a kind of fugue state. It takes a while to reach that point is what I'm saying, and until I do, my writing experience is one of neurotic editing and revision. I'm torn between the best approach for producing my fiction. I find the act of writing with pen more satisfying than typing because I have a physical record that in a way signifies that, yes, I am breathing, I am producing, I am living. Digital records aren't "real" in this sense. Conversely, I physically type faster than I write, so I can draft and revise in real time and produce considerably more consistent writing this way than by hand. When I write by hand, I have to transcribe everything, which is then filtered by my editing/revising brain such that I sometimes end with an artifact that differs greatly from the source material.

I wonder. What is real?

One thing troubles me today: AI has made human authenticity obsolete. I see it in the scripts people write for YouTube video essays. Is it authentic to write an essay and have something like ChatGPT review and "improve" it? The creator named Jacksfilms recently had a livestream where he interacted with his viewers by watching and constructively critiquing their content. One of the creators (whose name escapes me) wrote film reviews, and Jack and his audience clocked the use of AI fairly quickly. Specifically, ChatGPT's excessive use of the phrase, "That's not just X; that's also Y" or some derivative of it. The author, who said they struggled with English, explained that they wrote out their ideas by hand, transcribed them, then plugged them into ChatGPT to help improve the quality. Jack, who views AI as a tool, responded by saying that it was exactly the flaws that lend authenticity to a creator's work, and by using AI to "improve" their script, the AI simply stripped out the author's unique voice and replaced it with something much colder.

What we crave is feedback, us creators. I admit to using AI in several ways. In this, I align with Jack: AI is a useful tool that can aid in the more finicky sides of creating any kind of content. For Jack, this is most helpful in photoshop, where the AI is great at detecting the subject of a photograph or video still frame; likewise, I find value in treating AI tools as a kind of living journal. I can interrogate the text from a perspective I don't have when I'm too close to it. I can better understand where something I write might be perceived in a way that doesn't reflect what I'm trying to write. This in turn helps me identify better language to use. But I also see the temptation to offload all aspects of creation to something that doesn't have to think, like I do. Like we do. Unfortunately, I fear that the fight over AI on ethical and moral grounds has failed at a conceptual level because it has become deeply engrained in our culture and psyche in only a few short years. It is pervasive now, and it is already sowing distrust among creators. Where should creators stand on this subject? I genuinely don't have an answer.

For example, it is my belief that AI is but another tool being heralded as the progenitor of doomsday. There is something in this argument that doesn't follow from its premises, but it is growing more and more challenging to identify where exactly this failure occurs. But we have definitely seen this before. It was the assembly line which started the kind of automation that was meant to destroy manufacturing as a concept, and that might be true to some extent. Then again, it also led to the adoption of safer work practices and better processes, which have both contributed to humanity's ability to provide for everyone globally. Then again, manufacturing is practically invisible to the average American, for example; our towns no longer run on manufacturing, and our grand industries are largely failing because there is no point of entry for the poor intellectual to realistically compete. Combine with that the rampant greed of the wealthy ruling class currently controlling entire governments, and one starts to suspect that the structure of capital is a gilded balloon long ago popped. It will take but one finger pressed against the golden shell to break into the hollow, empty center. What will we find inside it? Nothing. The institutions, and the veneer of them, are failing.

I wanted to correct an overestimation in my progress update from last week. There was an issue in the way I shared the data. As previously described, I've started to include a description of where I expect the scene I was writing to go in a set of square brackets "[]" at the end of each writing session. When I included the figures for last week's progress, I hadn't accounted for these descriptions taking up space. As such, the figure of 2859 words was misleading; I actually wrote 2583 words of prose. This week, I made sure to take this into consideration. I am currently sitting at 6131 words of prose, which means I wrote approximately 3600 words this week. Still off pace, but viewed another way, I'm nearly one-tenth of the way through my draft. Good progress for two weeks, I reckon.

Now, I still haven't renewed my commitment to writing a poem a day, but I have good reason. Actually, I'll let you be the judge of that. When I was writing a poem a day, I started using that as an excuse to avoid working on my novel. The justification was simple: I already wrote today. I do intend to revisit this project very soon, but first, I want to establish a habit of writing forward on my novel. Any poetry I write will therefore have to be written only after I've worked on my novel. I think this is good practice.

And speaking more broadly of writing exercises, I've been listening to the lectures of Dr. John Pistelli (those available on his YouTube page), particularly on the topics of postmodernism and beyond. It's a subject I've found elusive, and I still struggle to describe the movement plainly. But I feel its meaning to some extent. I've immersed myself in the literature of its writers for a decade or more, so I recognize its tenets of irony and deconstruction in my own beliefs and habits. The writer Charles Johnson once said (roughly; I'm paraphrasing from memory) that all good writers must believe in something, which he reaffirms by saying that there are no good writers who believe in nothing. This was in a vaguely religious context, and I believe it was a reaction to the postmodernist's rejection of grand narratives. Johnson is an excellent writer. His book "The Way of the Writer" demonstrates his writing lessons rather than explaining them; it's an instructive, informative read and the source of the paraphrased quote. I understand his rejection of postmodernism, but I reject the language he uses. I, for one, don't believe in the grand narratives of history or religion, yet I write. I may not be a great writer like Johnson, but I'm certainly not a bad writer. And I write despite my lack of belief.

It was on this question of beliefs that Pistelli's lectures provided an avenue to explore Johnson's claims in myself. Maybe I'm coming around, I don't know. Anyway, I learned about many writers who embraced the form of the manifesto to question and challenge their own ideas and to posit a definition of themselves as artists, and so I've taken this as an opportunity to explore the form by writing one for myself. About myself. It is a personal statement, presently, but perhaps I'll share it one day.

So in summary, it has been a productive, long, slow week. The drought finally broke yesterday, long after the swollen clouds first appeared, and the smattering of rain patter outside my writing room window is soothing. The chickens seem to be happy about it too. This morning, I sat on the porch with my wife, listening to the rain and watching the yellow garden spider we named Good Charlotte reconstructing its storm-damaged web. It swung back and forth like a pendulum from a strand of silk so thin it seemed to float instead, until it curled back, grabbed the slack line, and climbed, climbed, climbed to the safety of the wind-breaking rafter.

Weekly highlight: The votives were lit and flickering once more, and their light cast tiny dancing shadows on the feet of Christ. I didn't need to burn His temple, He was burning Himself.

Until next week,

JD

Ruminance Blog #001 - September 25, 2026

It's Friday, September 25, 2026. Approximately noon. I've finished writing for the day. For the week, I reckon; my weeks lately run Saturday to Friday. The days sort of shift for me, in terms of beginnings and endings. Days aren't real anymore, nor are weeks or months. Just liminal stretches of time that waver between periods of practical usefulness and something akin to hibernation. I've been dormant for a while.

Sad news for a first blog of this kind, but I've archived approximately 20,000 words again because the vision for my novel has similarly shifted. Maybe saying the vision has shifted is wrong, after all I'm still writing about the same characters in the same setting and time period, but the characters have certainly shifted into unrecognizable versions of themselves. A lot of that has to do with the shift in eras. I could talk for ages about the settings I've been exploring. It's a bummer that so much research must be set aside, but frankly, whatever choice makes the story work is the right choice. I'm at least as confident in that as I am that my previous choices weren't working and were therefore the wrong choices to make.

Now according to the stat tracker in Novlr, I've written 2859 words over the last few days. My goal is 70,000 by January. I'm a little off pace, but there's some buffer built in. For one, I planned to take weekends off to focus on household chores, homework, meal prep. I've been distracted by a big project to try and degoogle my house (this is a tremendous effort, actually, despite the proliferation of open source alternatives). This meant dusting off my old networking and IT skills to repair and restore several old computers into functional server hubs. I swear, I won't dwell on this, but it's difficult to feel safe in my own home when I'm surrounded by technology that might be spying on me. I feel like an addict sometimes. Technology has a tight grip on me, so taking control of my home network and restricting what data leaves it has been a challenging but rewarding endeavor.

Back to the writing.

This time around, I've decided to trust my discovery process and lean into it, rather than trying to impose a rigid approach to drafting that is doomed to fail. I have an outline, and I know pretty certainly what is going to happen, who my characters are, and how they will change, but confining myself to filling out the steps between them until I hit a word count has been seriously detrimental to my creative process. So I'm experimenting. This week's experiment: at the end of each day's "discovery draft", I attach a bracketed "[]" footer that describes where I expect the scene, chapter, or draft to go from where I leave off. I'm eager to see how this pays off in the weeks and months to come and if it will at all.

This week, as I've already mentioned, I have begun again. This is my third revision so far, scoped down tremendously from a planned trilogy of revenge to a much more hollowed (and hallowed?) single novel told in first person. With this fresh start (granted, I have workable material that I suspect I can salvage from the now-archived revision 2), I have started a new prologue with the confession frame structure strictly in mind; I have written a new shakedown scene to replace a half-baked previous version; I have started writing a brand new scene where my protagonist goes on a date with the woman he expects to marry. I'm hoping these footers will act both as springboards for drafting new scenes and bridges for connecting them.

Aside from the work on my novel, I have done little to no writing. I have made a few Reddit comments, but that's about it. I have been brainstorming an idea for a game that I might actually be able to create on my own, but I have neither the time nor the energy to dedicate to it right now. I'm still wanting to learn more about writing interactive fiction using something like Twine, but I've found it too limiting and tricky to use for the more complex mechanics I'd like to use like persistent inventories and character sheets. I need to study this more.

Looking forward, I have a few goals in mind. First is to get full marks for my current class to further raise my GPA after last year's winter term, which was emotionally difficult. Next is to utilize the Tarot cards I purchased as the story generators they are, which I should probably leverage to write more poetry since I've been lacking in my "daily poem" exercise.

Now for a highlight from this week's discovery drafts: Metzgersohn was a bald, squat man, square in proportion and alarmingly vascular.

Until next week,

JD
