Etherized, Indeed

Review of Major Arcana by John Pistelli.

The diction of my high school daze was, “Dude, whassup?” Dude was the first all-gendered word we knew to use, directly in protest of “Hey, guys,” but its first frictions existed from the beginning. Some, not many, but not a few, insisted that dude was purely masculine, that its use was impolite at all, much less for girls. Truly, it was just easier to use than the distinctions between boy and girl, between he and she. It was impolite for there to be mixed company, and the older teachers in our schools couldn’t tell the difference between all the new labels we’d created for ourselves. Our mothers knew of boys who’d changed genders. Some, not many, but not a few, had even changed sexes. “They’re so brave,” they’d say. Or, “I just don’t get it, but you do you.”

Often, however, we’d hear the dreaded line, “That just didn’t happen in my day.” We’d hear it, and we’d argue about love. We’d argue about religion, argue about science, literature, and philosophy, argue about meaning; we’d argue, argue, argue. And arguing felt like it meant something. We were people deeply wounded and curious, arguing about the increasingly complicated world and its seemingly endless expanse of cruelty and resolving through ancient human means our little crises. We’d see past our hurting selves and look, closely, at the humans sharing our suffering, and we’d think but not say, I love you, I’m sorry, we’ll be okay.

But this mutual understanding dissolved into meaningless pedantry. The individual he/she replaced the communal we, and the society that once existed collapsed under the weight of a new, indecipherable social contract. The kids have known this. The kids were born in it. Some of their older cousins, not many, but not a few, recognized it too, but were powerless to stop the change. The kids, rejecting the solitary for the social, rejected the individual he/him/she/her for the communal they/them, much to the derision of their grandparents and the delight of their equally powerless parents.

John Pistelli’s novel Major Arcana chronicles a cast of characters as they navigate the horrific implications of the late 20th century’s dissolution of the self. Largely centered on Simon Magnus’ magnum opus Overman 3000, it follows his life from boyhood to retirement through the eyes of many other characters in his orbit, most notably the women. Pistelli reaches equally for Milton, Melville, Morrison, and Moore, balancing centuries of influences spanning genre and form, to render these lives quietly, without judgement or resentment. The effect is a dizzying spiral made mesmerizing by the coven of women, Magnus sometimes included, independently redefining womanhood, then personhood, then finally humanity itself until all meaning is permanently displaced, obliterated, forever leaving behind a void awaiting fulfillment. The reader is Pistelli’s patient, etherized upon Eliot’s table.

It is Simon Magnus’ long-time lover, Ellen Chandler, who shattered his delusions. Genius or not, she told him, he relied on women to carry him forward in time and give him life. He wrote violently about sex and resilient women and fragile masculinity, while modeling himself after the women who collectively mothered him throughout his years. He wrote without caution or restraint and received glowering praise. He indulged his fans and fantasies, and fought for his right to live as a man without gender, a man above gender. Ultimately, he lived to see the ruin of his making in the life of Ash del Greco, the young woman he taught briefly in university. The young woman who filmed a young man’s suicide and posted it on YouTube. The young woman who claimed, in the very same video, that Simon Magnus was her all-gender, all-parent. Major Arcana is a story of impossibility, a story of motherhood.
